Why it is priced low

  • The headline price per square foot is misleading. At $289,999 it works out to $111 a square foot on all 2,608 finished square feet, against about $170 for recent Lake Saint Louis sales (Resideline, six months to late August 2026). Count only the 1,380 square feet above ground and it is $210 a square foot. Appraisers and buyers value basement space well below main-floor space.
  • Three of the four bedrooms are in the basement. The county record counts one bedroom above ground, so a family with small children sleeps on two different floors.
  • The interior needs flooring, paint and ceiling work throughout, and the seller will not do any of it (sold as-is).
  • The seller is an investor who paid $180,000 in April 2024. Even at $289,999 the investor clears roughly $110,000 before the roof, holding costs and commission, so there is room in the price.
  • A contract already failed once, which makes later buyers nervous, and the price was cut afterwards.

Problems and risks

Stained, worn carpet nearly everywhere

Dark stains and traffic wear run through the living room (photos 4 to 7), the blue main bedroom (photo 11) and the whole lower level (photos 14 to 16). Spot patterns like these are often pets. Plan on pulling all of it and checking the subfloor and the concrete underneath for odor or moisture.

Cost: Estimated $7,000 to $14,000 for about 1,700 square feet, from typical St. Louis installed prices of $4 to $8 a square foot for carpet or vinyl plank.

Unfinished drywall patch in the main bedroom

A large section of the blue wall is patched and left unpainted (photo 11). Ask what was behind it: a leak, a hole or a moved outlet. A patch this size near windows can be a past water problem.

Cost: A few hundred dollars to finish, unless it hides water damage.

Plywood patch under the upper deck

The underside of the upper deck, right against the house, has a plywood patch over the boards (photo 25). That is where the deck attaches to the house, the most common spot for hidden rot and leaks into the wall. Have the inspector check the deck ledger, its flashing and the posts on both tiers.

Cost: Unknown until inspected. Minor flashing work runs hundreds; a two-tier deck rebuild can pass $20,000.

Water management on a sloped, wooded lot

Black corrugated drain pipes run from the downspouts out into the yard on both sides (photos 26 and 27), and the ground under the trees is bare dirt. Someone has been working to move water away from a walkout basement. Ask whether the basement has ever taken water, and look at the lower-level drywall and carpet edges for staining.

Cost: Grading or buried drain lines usually run $1,000 to $5,000 (estimate).

Capped plumbing in the half bath

The half bath has a capped pipe or valve on the wall beside the toilet (photo 17). A shower or tub may have been removed. If that half bath is the one in the basement, the three basement bedrooms have no shower on their floor.

Cost: Ask before pricing. Adding a shower back is roughly $5,000 to $12,000 (estimate).

Textured popcorn ceilings throughout

Every room in the photos has a sprayed texture ceiling. On a 1978 house that texture may contain asbestos. Test it before anyone scrapes it.

Cost: A lab test is about $50 to $100 a sample. Removal runs about $1 to $2 a square foot if it is clean and $3 to $7 if it needs abatement (estimates).

Unknown furnace, air conditioner and water heater

The listing names forced-air heat and central air with no ages. An investor listing that brags about a 2024 roof but stays quiet on the mechanicals usually means they are old. Ask for photos of the data plates.

Cost: Furnace and air conditioner together run $8,000 to $14,000; a water heater runs $1,500 to $2,500 (estimates).

Electrical panel listed as Other

The listing gives the electric service as Other. Houses from the 1970s sometimes carry Federal Pacific or Zinsco panels, which insurers dislike. Ask for a photo of the panel label.

Cost: A panel swap runs about $2,000 to $4,000 if needed (estimate).

Big trees close to the house

Mature oaks stand over the roof and around the back (photos 1, 26 and 27). Expect gutter clogging, limbs over the new roof, and roots near the sewer line. Get the sewer line camera-scoped.

Cost: A sewer scope is about $150 to $300. Tree trimming over the roof is often $500 to $2,000 (estimates).

Radon in the basement bedrooms

St. Charles County sits in the federal moderate radon category, and three bedrooms are below ground. Test before closing.

Cost: A test is $15 to $150. A mitigation fan system usually runs $1,000 to $1,500 if the reading is high (estimate).

Taxes will likely rise

The county raised the assessed value 26.8 percent in 2023 and 7.7 percent in 2025. Missouri reassesses in odd years, and a sale near $290,000 sits well above the county's current value of $246,930.

Cost: $3,301 for 2025. Expect more after the 2027 reassessment.

Ask before making an offer

  1. Why did the September contract fall through? Ask for the buyer's inspection report or the list of items that buyer asked to have fixed.
  2. The seller is a licensed agent with an ownership interest, so ask for a full seller's disclosure statement, not an investor's blank one.
  3. What was behind the drywall patch in the main bedroom?
  4. Has the basement ever taken water? What do the corrugated drain pipes connect to?
  5. Where is the half bath, and what was the capped line on its wall?
  6. Ages of the furnace, air conditioner and water heater, with data-plate photos.
  7. Brand of the electrical panel and the water supply pipe material.
  8. Permits for the roof, the basement finish and any walls moved on the main floor.
  9. Was the house rented between 2024 and 2026, and is it vacant now?
  10. Copy of the association covenants and any transfer or move-in fees.
